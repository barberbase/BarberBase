A barbershop rarely gets to choose one kind of customer. Some people walk in off the street. Some join the queue from their phone before they've even left home. Some book an appointment for later in the day. A tool built around only one of these breaks the moment real customers show up in the other mode.

## The problem with treating them separately

Systems built for appointments alone struggle with walk-ins, because a walk-in doesn't fit into a pre-booked slot — they're standing in the shop right now. Systems built for a physical line alone struggle with remote joins, because there's no way for someone to hold a spot without being physically present. Shops end up running two informal systems side by side: a paper list for whoever's in the chair area, and a separate mental note for anyone who called ahead.

## How BarberBase handles it

There's one queue, and customers can enter it two ways. A customer can walk in and join from their phone by scanning a QR code or tapping a link — no different from joining remotely, except they happen to already be at the shop. Or they can join before arriving, from wherever they are, and travel to the shop once they're near the front of the line.

Either way, the queue entry looks the same to staff: a position, an estimated wait, and a status. The staff dashboard doesn't have a separate view for "people who walked in" versus "people who joined remotely" — it's one board with call next, start, complete, and walk-in actions that work the same regardless of how someone entered the line.

Appointments sit on the same system too. A customer who books a service and time slot gets a WhatsApp confirmation, and when their slot arrives, they move through the same dashboard as a walk-in or a remote join.

## Why this matters for a real shop

A shop with a small waiting area benefits the most from this. If every remote-joined customer still has to be present to hold their spot, the room fills up the same way it would with a pure walk-in line. Letting people join remotely and travel to the shop as their turn approaches is what actually reduces crowding — not just tracking position digitally while everyone still has to stand there.

It also means a shop doesn't have to explain two different processes to customers. Whether someone found the shop by walking past it or by clicking a shared link, the instructions are the same: scan or tap to join, track your position, get a WhatsApp message when it's time, then confirm you're on your way and confirm arrival with the counter PIN when you're there.

One line, one dashboard, and a queue that doesn't care how a customer got into it — that's the difference between running one tool and running two workarounds that happen to share a waiting room.
